The $p$-adic Integers

An interactive introduction

Background: this introduction assumes familiarity with basic abstract algebra, including groups and rings.

1. Numbers that keep going to the left

Fix a prime $p$. Every non-negative integer has a base-$p$ expansion $$n = a_0 + a_1 p + a_2 p^2 + \cdots + a_k p^k, \qquad a_i \in \{0, 1, \ldots, p-1\},$$ and we usually write the digits from right to left: $n = a_k \ldots a_2\, a_1\, a_0$. For example, with $p = 5$, $$38 = 3 + 2\cdot 5 + 1\cdot 5^2 = 123_{(5)}.$$

The expansion of a non-negative integer always stops. What if we let it continue forever to the left? A $p$-adic integer is a formal series $$x = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} a_i p^i = a_0 + a_1 p + a_2 p^2 + \cdots, \qquad a_i \in \{0, 1, \ldots, p-1\},$$ which we write as $\ldots a_3\, a_2\, a_1\, a_0$. The set of all of them is written $\mathbb{Z}_p$. As section 2 shows, some of the infinite digit strings can be identified with familiar types of numbers, like negative integers and fractions.

A sum with infinitely many non-zero terms looks as if it must be infinitely large. But we never add up the terms to get a size. A $p$-adic integer is its string of digits, and the series notation is a convenient way to name the digits and their positions. What makes these strings interesting is that we can do arithmetic with them. In grade-school addition and multiplication, the last $n$ digits of the answer depend only on the last $n$ digits of the inputs. So every digit of a sum or product is computed after finitely many steps, even though there are infinitely many digits.

2. Two representations of the same number

The series representation describes a $p$-adic integer by its digits $a_0, a_1, a_2, \ldots$. The sequence representation describes it by what you see when you cut it off after $n$ digits: $$x_n = a_0 + a_1 p + \cdots + a_{n-1}p^{n-1}, \qquad 0 \le x_n \lt p^n.$$ Each $x_n$ is a residue modulo $p^n$. Going from $x_n$ to $x_{n+1}$ adds one more term of the series, and that term is a multiple of $p^n$, so consecutive terms agree as far as they go: $$\begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= x_n + a_n p^n \\ &\equiv x_n \pmod{p^n}. \end{aligned}$$ We call a sequence $(x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots)$ with $0 \le x_n \lt p^n$ and this property a coherent sequence. Going back is just as easy: solve the same equation for the digit, $$a_0 = x_1, \qquad a_n = \frac{x_{n+1} - x_n}{p^n}.$$ So the two representations describe exactly the same objects.

Beyond the non-negative integers

For a non-negative integer $m$, the term $x_n$ is just $m \bmod p^n$, and once $p^n > m$ every term equals $m$: the digits stop. The sequence representation suggests what the other $p$-adic integers might be. All we need is a sensible value for "the number modulo $p^n$" for every $n$.

The integer $-1$. Modulo $p^n$, the residue of $-1$ is $x_n = p^n - 1$. Just as $10^n - 1 = 99\ldots9$ in base ten, $p^n - 1$ is written in base $p$ with $n$ digits, all equal to $p - 1$: $$p^n - 1 = (p-1) + (p-1)p + \cdots + (p-1)p^{n-1}.$$ Every term of the sequence adds one more digit $p - 1$, so $$-1 = \ldots (p-1)(p-1)(p-1).$$ For $p = 5$ the sequence is $4, 24, 124, 624, \ldots$, and $-1 = \ldots 4444_{(5)}$.

The fraction $-\tfrac14$. Take $p = 5$. Because $4$ is invertible modulo every power of $5$, the fraction $-\tfrac14$ has a residue modulo $5^n$: the unique $x_n$ with $4x_n \equiv -1 \pmod{5^n}$. $$4 \cdot 1 = 4 \equiv -1 \!\!\pmod 5, \qquad 4 \cdot 6 = 24 \equiv -1 \!\!\pmod{25}, \qquad 4 \cdot 31 = 124 \equiv -1 \!\!\pmod{125}, \qquad \ldots$$ So the sequence is $1, 6, 31, 156, \ldots$, that is, $x_n = (5^n - 1)/4$. In base $5$ these are $1, 11, 111, 1111, \ldots$, so $$-\tfrac14 = \ldots 1111_{(5)}.$$ The same works for any fraction whose denominator is not divisible by $p$; section 5 explains why. Most $p$-adic integers, though, are neither integers nor fractions. There are only countably many fractions, but uncountably many infinite digit strings.

For algebraists. The sequences live in the direct product $\prod_{n \ge 1} \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$, but coherence is an extra condition: $x_{n+1}$ must map to $x_n$ under the reduction map $\mathbb{Z}/p^{n+1}\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$. The coherent sequences form the inverse limit $\varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$, a subring of the product. You may also see $\mathbb{Z}_p$ defined as equivalence classes of integer sequences satisfying the congruence. The sequences with $0 \le x_n \lt p^n$ used here are the canonical representatives of those classes.

3. $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a ring

In the series representation, using digits, we add and multiply exactly as in grade school. Carries move to the left, and there is no last column where they have to stop. Digit $k$ of $x + y$ or $xy$ depends only on digits $0, \ldots, k$ of $x$ and $y$, so both operations are well defined on infinite digit strings.

In the sequence representation, the ring structure is even clearer. Operate term by term: $$(x_n) + (y_n) = \big((x_n + y_n) \bmod p^n\big), \qquad (x_n)\cdot(y_n) = \big(x_n y_n \bmod p^n\big).$$ Each $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ is a ring, and reduction modulo $p^n$ respects $+$ and $\times$, so the result is again coherent. So $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is closed under both operations and inherits the ring axioms from the rings $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$. It is a commutative ring with identity $$1 = \ldots 0001 = (1, 1, 1, \ldots).$$

The demo computes the same sum and product in both representations. You can enter fractions such as $2/3$ as well as integers.

4. The integers are a subring of $\mathbb{Z}_p$

The map $$\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}_p, \qquad n \mapsto (n \bmod p,\ n \bmod p^2,\ n \bmod p^3, \ldots)$$ is a ring homomorphism. It is injective: if $n \ne 0$, then $n \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p^k}$ as soon as $p^k > |n|$. So $\mathbb{Z}$ is a subring of $\mathbb{Z}_p$.

For $n \ge 0$, the $p$-adic digits of $n$ are its base-$p$ digits followed by zeros. In other words, there is an $N$ with $a_m = 0$ for all $m > N$, and the formal series is just the base-$p$ representation. Negative integers are different:

Section 2 found the digits of $-1$ from its residues. Another way to find the digits of $-n$ is to ask what must be added to $n$ to get $0$. Work it out yourself, one column at a time from the right.

Try it: find the digits of $-n$

The pattern is the same for every $n > 0$. Choose $k$ with $p^k > n$. Modulo $p^k$ the residue of $-n$ is $p^k - n$, and each further power of $p$ contributes a digit $p - 1$, exactly as for $-1$: $$p^m - n = (p^k - n) + (p-1)p^k + (p-1)p^{k+1} + \cdots + (p-1)p^{m-1} \qquad (m \ge k).$$ So the digits of $-n$ are the $k$ base-$p$ digits of $p^k - n$, followed by $p - 1$ forever. For example, with $p = 5$ and $n = 7$, take $k = 2$: $25 - 7 = 18 = 33_{(5)}$, so $-7 = \ldots 44433_{(5)}$.

The integers inside $\mathbb{Z}_p$ are therefore exactly the digit strings that end in all $0$s (the non-negative integers) or all $(p-1)$s (the negative integers).

5. Division, and the units of $\mathbb{Z}_p$

Recall that a unit in a ring is an element with a multiplicative inverse: $u$ is a unit if there is some $v$ in the ring with $uv = 1$. In $\mathbb{Z}$ the only units are $1$ and $-1$. For example, $3$ is not a unit, because no integer $q$ satisfies $3q = 1$.

$\mathbb{Z}_p$ has many more units. Here is a challenge: find the inverse of $3$, a $p$-adic integer $q$ with $3 \cdot q = 1$. Build it yourself one digit at a time, starting from the right.

Try it: build $q = a / b$ digit by digit

What just happened. Suppose you have already found $q_0, \ldots, q_{k-1}$, so the remainder $a - bq$ ends in $k$ zeros. Choosing $q_k$ changes $bq$ by $q_k b\, p^k$, and the only part of that which touches position $k$ is $q_k b_0$. So you need $$b_0\, q_k \equiv r_k \pmod p,$$ where $r_k$ is the digit of the remainder at position $k$. Because $p$ is prime, $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field. If $b_0 \ne 0$, this congruence has exactly one solution $q_k = r_k b_0^{-1} \bmod p$, at every step and forever. If $b_0 = 0$, then $b q$ always ends in $0$, so it can never equal $1$.

Theorem. A $p$-adic integer $x = \ldots a_2 a_1 a_0$ is a unit in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ if and only if $a_0 \ne 0$, that is, if and only if $p \nmid x$.

Some consequences:

  • Every fraction $c/b$ with $p \nmid b$ is a $p$-adic integer, namely $c \cdot b^{-1}$. Its digits are always eventually periodic, just like the decimal expansion of a fraction. (The converse is also true: an eventually periodic digit string is a fraction.) Try $a = 1,\ b = 1 - p$ for your chosen $p$ and compare the answer with section 4.
  • Every non-zero $x \in \mathbb{Z}_p$ can be written uniquely as $x = p^v u$ with $u$ a unit. Shift away the $v$ zeros at the right-hand end and what is left has a non-zero last digit.
  • The non-units are exactly the multiples of $p$, so they form an ideal $p\mathbb{Z}_p$. It is the unique maximal ideal: $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a local ring, with residue field $\mathbb{Z}_p / p\mathbb{Z}_p \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.

6. The $p$-adic valuation

The number $v$ in $x = p^v u$ deserves a name. The $p$-adic valuation of a non-zero integer is the number of times $p$ divides it: $$v_p(n) = \max\{k : p^k \mid n\}, \qquad v_p(0) = \infty,$$ and for fractions $v_p(c/b) = v_p(c) - v_p(b)$, which can be negative. For a $p$-adic integer, $v_p(x)$ is the position of the lowest non-zero digit. Two properties drive everything: $$v_p(xy) = v_p(x) + v_p(y), \qquad v_p(x + y) \ge \min\big(v_p(x), v_p(y)\big).$$ The second is the strong triangle inequality. Its proof is one line: if $p^k$ divides both $x$ and $y$, it divides $x + y$. Moreover, if $v_p(x) \ne v_p(y)$ the inequality is an equality, because the lowest non-zero digit of the less divisible term cannot be cancelled.

Closure under addition. The fractions that are $p$-adic integers are exactly those with non-negative valuation: $$\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} = \{\, c/b \in \mathbb{Q} : p \nmid b \,\} = \{\, x \in \mathbb{Q} : v_p(x) \ge 0 \,\}.$$ If $v_p(x) \ge 0$ and $v_p(y) \ge 0$, the strong triangle inequality and the product rule give $$v_p(x+y) \ge \min\big(v_p(x), v_p(y)\big) \ge 0, \qquad v_p(xy) = v_p(x) + v_p(y) \ge 0.$$ So this set is closed under addition and multiplication: it is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$. The same argument, applied to the field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ of $p$-adic numbers (fractions of $p$-adic integers), shows that $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is the set of elements of valuation $\ge 0$ in $\mathbb{Q}_p$.

Try it: valuations of a sum

Going further: $p$-adic distance. Setting $|x|_p = p^{-v_p(x)}$ (and $|0|_p = 0$) turns the valuation into an absolute value, in which numbers divisible by a high power of $p$ are small. The strong triangle inequality becomes $|x + y|_p \le \max(|x|_p, |y|_p)$, which is stronger than the ordinary triangle inequality. Two $p$-adic integers are close when their last digits agree: if $x$ and $y$ share their last $n$ digits, then $|x - y|_p \le p^{-n}$.

7. Read the code

Every computation on this page is done by a small TypeScript library in src/padic/. Each file covers one idea:

  • digits.ts: integers to $p$-adic digits (the series representation)
  • sequences.ts: coherent sequences (the sequence representation) and conversion to and from digits
  • arithmetic.ts: addition with carries, long multiplication
  • inverse.ts: right-to-left long division and units
  • rational.ts: fractions and their eventually periodic expansions
  • valuation.ts: $v_p$, the strong triangle inequality, $|x|_p$
  • modular.ts: small helpers (remainders, powers, inverses mod $p$)

8. Further reading

Everything on this page is standard material, found in the opening chapters of most introductions to $p$-adic numbers. These are good places to continue.

  1. Fernando Q. Gouvêa, $p$-adic Numbers: An Introduction, 3rd ed., Universitext, Springer, 2020. doi:10.1007/978-3-030-47295-5. The friendliest full-length introduction. It develops $\mathbb{Z}_p$ through digit expansions and coherent sequences, much as this page does, and continues to analysis and algebra over $\mathbb{Q}_p$.
  2. Svetlana Katok, $p$-adic Analysis Compared with Real, Student Mathematical Library 37, American Mathematical Society, 2007. AMS bookstore page. Short and elementary. Its first chapter covers the arithmetic of $p$-adic numbers, and the rest keeps comparing them with the real numbers.
  3. Neal Koblitz, $p$-adic Numbers, $p$-adic Analysis, and Zeta-Functions, 2nd ed., Graduate Texts in Mathematics 58, Springer, 1984. doi:10.1007/978-1-4612-1112-9. Chapter I is a concise treatment of the valuation, $\mathbb{Q}_p$, and computing $p$-adic expansions, with many exercises.
  4. Jean-Pierre Serre, A Course in Arithmetic, Graduate Texts in Mathematics 7, Springer, 1973. doi:10.1007/978-1-4684-9884-4. Chapter II defines $\mathbb{Z}_p$ as the inverse limit of the rings $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ and characterizes its units in a few pages. It is terse, and suits readers comfortable with algebra.
  5. Keith Conrad, “The $p$-adic expansion of rational numbers,” expository notes. PDF. Proves that the rational numbers are exactly the $p$-adic numbers with eventually periodic expansions, and gives an algorithm for computing them (compare section 5).
